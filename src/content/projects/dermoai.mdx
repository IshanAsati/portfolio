DermoAI started as a skin-image classifier: a ConvNeXt-Base model that sorts a photo into one of twelve categories. It is now an Android app built around that model. You photograph a patch of skin, the app checks the photo, runs the model on the phone, and explains the result in plain language with a clear next step. The app also keeps a photo timeline, daily check-ins, routines and a report you can take to a doctor.

The app is in active development. There is a working debug build, but no Play Store release.

## Overview

The project has two halves.

The **research model** is the original work. I fine-tuned ConvNeXt-Base on twelve skin categories and evaluated it with test-time augmentation. That entry placed first in the DAV ML Khanna machine learning competition. Its training code, weights and tester live in the [research repository](https://github.com/IshanAsati/dermoai-final).

The **Android app** puts that model in a phone and builds a product around it. The project placed third in Zone 21 of the CBSE DOE Science Exhibition 2026-27, under Science: Artificial Intelligence (AI) for Better Life. The app's source is in the [Android repository](https://github.com/IshanAsati/dermoai-android).

Neither result is evidence that the model is ready for clinical use.

## The problem

A model that scores well on its validation split answers a narrow question. An app has to answer a harder one: what happens when a real person points a real phone at their own skin, in bad light, and reads the result alone?

Moving to the phone exposed problems the research numbers never showed:

- **Real photos are not dataset photos.** Phone pictures are blurry, dark, tilted and taken at odd distances.
- **The model had never really learned healthy skin.** Its Healthy class scored well on its own validation split. On real phone photos of normal skin it almost always predicted a lesion instead.
- **A label is not guidance.** "SCC, 71%" is frightening and unhelpful. The app has to turn a prediction into a calm, honest next step without overstating what the model knows.

## What the app does

The scan flow is the core of the app:

1. **Capture.** A CameraX screen with a framing guide, or a photo from the gallery.
2. **Crop.** Manual crop and rotate, so the model sees the skin and not the room.
3. **Quality check.** Before analysis, the app measures blur (variance of a Laplacian filter), exposure (mean brightness) and resolution. A poor photo gets a warning and guidance. You can retake, adjust or analyse anyway; the check never blocks you.
4. **Inference.** The model runs on the phone with TensorFlow Lite.
5. **Healthy-skin gate.** A small second model decides whether the photo looks like normal skin (more on this below).
6. **Profile rules.** A rule engine uses the profile you set up (age, skin type, sun exposure and similar) to adjust the level of concern. The rules can raise concern but never lower it, and the app lists every adjustment it made.
7. **Guidance.** The result maps to one of four tiers: educational, monitor, consult or urgent. High-concern results show a button that opens a map of nearby dermatologists.

Around the scan, the app includes:

- A photo **timeline** for tracking a spot over time.
- **SkinMind**, a daily check-in with optional voice notes.
- **Treatment routines**, plus a wellness section with a breathing exercise and a journal.
- **Analytics** with rule-based pattern hints, labelled "possible pattern — not medical advice".
- A **PDF doctor report** generated on the phone and shared through the system share sheet.
- **UV and heat alerts** from the Open-Meteo weather API.
- An offline **FAQ** on skin conditions, prevention and how the app works.
- A **dermatologist finder** built on OpenStreetMap data.
- An optional **AI chat assistant**. It only works if you paste in your own DeepSeek API key, so no key ships inside the app.
- English, with partial translations into Hindi, Marathi, Bengali, Telugu and Tamil.

## Architecture

The app is a multi-module Kotlin project with 24 Gradle modules: ten core modules and thirteen feature modules around a thin app shell.

```text
camera / gallery
    │
    ▼
crop + rotate
    │
    ▼
image-quality check (blur, exposure, size)
    │
    ▼
ConvNeXt-Base, TensorFlow Lite, 224×224
    │                 │
    │                 └── 1024-d features ──▶ healthy-skin gate
    ▼
12-class probabilities
    │
    ▼
profile rules (escalate only)
    │
    ▼
severity tier + plain-language guidance
```

- **UI:** Jetpack Compose with a custom design system, plus CameraX for capture.
- **State and data:** Hilt for dependency injection, Room for local storage and DataStore for settings.
- **On-device by default:** inference, photos, the timeline and PDF reports stay on the phone.
- **Network use is specific:** Location goes to Open-Meteo for weather and to OpenStreetMap's Overpass API for the finder. Chat text goes to DeepSeek only if you turn chat on. Firebase handles sign-in, and Appwrite handles doctor sharing.

## The model on the phone

The deployed model is a ConvNeXt-Base checkpoint from the research project. It was converted from PyTorch through Keras to TensorFlow Lite, with 8-bit weights, which brings it to about 89 MB. Input is a 224×224 image with standard ImageNet normalisation. The model returns two things: the twelve class scores and the 1024-dimensional feature vector the gate needs.

Two changes after conversion mattered more than the conversion itself:

- **A healthy-skin gate.** On eleven phone photos of healthy skin, the classifier on its own labelled one as healthy. To fix this, a logistic-regression gate now reads the model's internal features and decides whether the photo looks like normal skin. It catches most of those photos. That test set is one person's skin from one camera, and it probably overlaps the gate's training data, so I treat this as a working fix rather than a measured result.
- **Removing a melanoma bias.** The research model came with a setting that pushed melanoma scores down. On a small sample of public ISIC photos, removing it doubled the share of melanomas the model caught, so the app no longer uses it.

The research evaluation used test-time augmentation. The app currently runs a single pass per image; wiring TTA into the phone pipeline is still on the list.

## The doctor side

DermoAI includes a doctor mode. A doctor signs up, gets a pairing code and QR code, and a patient links to them with explicit consent. The doctor sees an urgency-ranked list of linked patients, each with trends, routine adherence and clinical notes. A patient can revoke access at any time, and an audit log records access. Sharing can also be protected by a passcode.

The same view exists as a web dashboard built with Next.js and Appwrite, tested with Vitest unit tests and Playwright end-to-end tests against a fake Appwrite server. Sharing scan photos with a linked doctor is in progress and not yet part of the published code.

## Results

The research model's competition entry reported **82.79% accuracy** across twelve classes and **87.30% sensitivity on the cancer-related classes**. Those are the figures from the project's own evaluation, and they are the ones I present. I have not added a dataset size, a comparison against other architectures or a clinical study to make them sound stronger.

The work placed **first in the DAV ML Khanna machine learning competition** and **third in Zone 21 at the CBSE Science Exhibition 2026-27**, under Science: Artificial Intelligence (AI) for Better Life.

| Measure | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Accuracy (research model) | 82.79% |
| Cancer-class sensitivity (research model) | 87.30% |
| Classes | 12 |
| Inference in the app | On-device, TensorFlow Lite |
| Automated tests (Android) | 214 JVM unit tests |
| Competition | First place, DAV ML Khanna |
| Exhibition | Third place, CBSE Science Exhibition 2026-27, Zone 21 |

Accuracy and sensitivity answer different questions. A model can be accurate overall while still missing the cases that matter most. Reporting both is a minimum, not a complete evaluation.

## Limitations

The limitations are the important part of this page.

- **No clinical validation.** The model has not been tested in a clinical setting, against a prospective patient population, or under the supervision of dermatologists.
- **No claim of diagnostic utility.** A class prediction is not a diagnosis. The model's outputs are uncalibrated research outputs, and the app frames them as educational estimates.
- **The model is weakest where it matters most.** Melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma are its least reliable classes.
- **The healthy-skin gate is barely tested.** It has been checked on one person and one camera. How it behaves across skin tones, devices and lighting is unmeasured.
- **Dataset bias is unresolved.** Skin-image datasets under-represent darker skin tones and rare conditions. For an app aimed at users in India, this is the most serious open problem, and this project does not solve it.
- **Not released.** There is a debug build, but no store release. The doctor-sharing backend needs a security review before it holds real patient data.
- **Competition results are not product results.** First place at DAV ML Khanna and third in Zone 21 are useful signals about the project relative to its entrants. They are not evidence of safety, fairness or readiness.

I would rather state these limits plainly than let a strong headline number imply more than the work supports.

## What I learned

The research model taught me transfer learning and evaluation hygiene. The app taught me that most of the difficulty sits after the model. A photo has to be good enough to judge. A healthy arm should not come back as cancer. A frightening label has to become a calm, honest next step. None of that shows up in a validation score.

It was also a team project, and the most important fix in the app, the healthy-skin gate, was Aarush's work. DermoAI made me more interested in evaluation than in leaderboard chasing, and it made me describe my own work the same way the app describes a result: state the result, state the scope, state what remains unknown.
